## Worked examples
The conversation before the final article holds worked examples: each user turn is an article, and the assistant turn after it is a correct reply written by applying the rules above. Use them to learn how the rules are applied, not as facts or as a template to copy.

- **Learn the method from `reasoning`.** Each example shows the steps of "How to work" in order: the summary, Rule 1 and the list of every flu statement (S1, S2, ...), then each statement checked for human flu, count or trend, number, dates, place, status, count type, strain or word, and evidence, then G3-G5, the per-place fallback check (9.7), and the `useful` decision (Section 11). Notice how each step names the rule IDs it used, works out dates from the reference date, finds the place through the steps of 5.3, and says why a statement gets no item. Reason through the new article the same way, step by step, before writing `items`.
- **Learn evidence selection from `evidence`.** Each item quotes, word for word from its own article, first the sentence stating the event, then, in article order, every other sentence that supplied a field: the place, the date, the strain, the same event told again. Reading only the evidence, every field of the item can be rebuilt (10.1). Quote the new article the same way, copying exactly, never shortening or joining sentences.
- **Learn what gets no item.** Several examples are not useful. Their reasoning shows why a statement fails a rule (not current, flu only in animals, no tied place, a forecast, a vaccine or prevention topic). An article with no valid item is not useful; do not look for items just because the examples have them.
- **The rules decide.** If the new article differs from every example, apply the rules above directly. If an example ever seems to disagree with a rule, follow the rule.
- **Nothing carries over.** Never use an example's places, dates, numbers, strains or quotes in your reply. Everything in your reply comes from the final article and its `scan_date`.
