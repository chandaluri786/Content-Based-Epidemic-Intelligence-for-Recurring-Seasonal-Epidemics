You read one news article and fill in a flu record. Follow the steps in order, then reply with one
JSON object only.

Before the new article you see solved examples: an article, then its correct answer. Each item's
evidence shows the sentences it came from. Study how each evidence sentence leads to the item's
kind, number, status, total_or_new, place and dates, and reason the same way for the new article.

The article text may be shortened. "[…]" marks text that was cut out and "[NO flu-keyword sentence in
article]" means no flu sentence was kept. Never guess what the cut text said. Use only the words you can
see.

STEP 1. THE ARTICLE DATE.
The article date is the scan date given with the article. Use it even when the article or its link
shows another date. Work every time phrase ("yesterday", "on Tuesday", "last week") out from the
article date (step 6). Never use your own knowledge to invent a date.

STEP 2. IS THE ARTICLE USEFUL?
The article is useful if it has at least one item that passes every rule in steps 2 to 5. Otherwise
it is not useful. A useful article always has items; a not-useful article never has any.

An item is a CURRENT statement about flu IN PEOPLE in a NAMED COUNTRY. It is one of these:
 1. People ill with flu: cases, deaths or hospital admissions. At least one person must be affected
    by flu of any strain or variant, or by a flu-related disease (seasonal flu, swine flu, bird flu,
    H5N1, H9N2). The first case or first death of the year counts. A small group counts (two
    ministers with flu), even in an article about something else.
 2. The level or direction of flu in a place: rising, falling, peaking, low, widespread, an epidemic
    declared, an early or bad season, "no cases yet" at the start of a season.
 3. A season that is active now: "the season has started", "the season is underway", "flu season is
    upon us", "as flu season takes hold", "we haven't hit the peak yet", "the peak season is over".
 4. Suspected flu: suspected cases ("on suspicion", "samples sent"), or flu-like illness for named
    people or a place when the article names no other cause or test.
 5. Vague amounts of illness or death that the article ties to flu: "several students with swine flu",
    "several deaths have been reported", "an extraordinary outbreak". These are trends (step 4).
Human cases of animal flu count (H5N1 in a farm worker, H9N2 in a child).

These are NOT items:
 - flu in animals only (poultry, cattle, pigs, dogs, horses, wild birds)
 - illnesses that are not flu: "stomach flu", norovirus, cat flu, Hib ("haemophilus influenzae"),
   and cold-type illness where the article names another cause or test (dengue, malaria, COVID)
 - a "mystery illness": an outbreak that the article calls unexplained and under investigation,
   without saying it is flu or suspected flu. (Flu-like illness that is otherwise unexplained is a
   suspected case: see "status" in step 4.)
 - a line that is not the article's own statement: a "read more" or related-link headline, a teaser or
   headline-only entry on a topic or listing page, a heading or table row of a ranking or index ("Top ten
   states with flu activity"), a caption. These can point to a flu story, but the story itself is not in
   the article.
 - opinion and first-person writing: "I think the worst is yet to come", a reader letter, an essay in
   which the writer mentions their own illness without a date
 - a look-back in the past tense: "there was an uptick", "Quebec experienced a late flu season", "he fell
   ill in January" when the period has ended (step 3)
 - flu named only in passing: in a list, a comparison, a metaphor, a nickname ("Spanish flu",
   "Kung flu"), a symptom or drug side effect, a photo caption, or a business, investment or
   vaccine-development remark
 - prevention only: vaccination, tips, clinics, vaccine coverage. If the same article also states a
   current flu item, the article is useful and only that item is recorded
 - forecasts: "the season is coming", "could be severe", "may peak in January", "likely to increase"
 - risk statements: "the risk to humans is low", "no person-to-person spread"
 - general seasonal facts: "flu usually peaks in winter", "the season runs from October to April"
 - the past (step 3)
 - worldwide or regional statements with no country ("the southern hemisphere", "across Asia")
 - an undated personal story that may be an old event retold, or an old event mentioned as
   background ("Tensions were heightened when a child ... was confirmed")
 - illness or hospital demand in general that the article does not tie to flu or flu-like illness
   ("a surge in viral illnesses", "rising demand for medical attention")
 - spam, ads, and pages whose text does not match the link
An article that has these and also a current item is still useful; just do not record these.

Other diseases. An article mainly about COVID-19, RSV, dengue or another disease is useful only if it
has a current flu statement of its own. Cases, hospitalized and deaths are written only when the
number is for flu alone, and only when it is current; never record another disease's numbers or a
combined total of flu and another disease. A TREND may mention flu together with other diseases when
it is current ("a 30% rise in flu and RSV", "a surge in RSV and the flu and Covid"): that is still a
flu trend.

Mixed sentences. When a sentence mixes a forecast with current news, keep only the current part.
"Flu activity is increasing and is expected to worsen" gives a trend with the words "increasing".

Borderline. When a careful reader could go either way, give your best answer and set borderline to
true, with the reason in "reason". Set borderline to true only when ANY of these applies:
 - a named person has flu and neither the article nor its setting (the event, the person's base) gives a
   place you can be sure of
 - a single case has no date
 - a self-reported case has no test ("the minister says he has flu")
 - the item's period ended 31 to 45 days before the article
 - "this season" or "this year" with no direction ("flu season is ramping up"), and nothing else in the
   article is a current flu item
 - the whole flu content is one loose line in an article about something else
In the earlier test about 60% of useful answers were flagged borderline; the flag must stay rare so that
it means something. Do not flag an article that has a clear count, a clear direction, or a clear place and
date. If useful and borderline are both true, still write the items.

Named person with flu. A named person who has flu counts, even a sportsperson, actor or singer, when the
article gives the place of the illness or of the person's current situation (the match, the film
location, the city they live in). If neither the article nor its setting gives a country, there is no
item. This is the one case where the place may come from the person's setting; it still needs a country
you are sure of.

STEP 3. IS IT CURRENT?
Check every item twice. Keep it only if it passes both.
 Check 1. Did its period end recently? The article date minus end_date should be 30 days or less.
   Measure from the END of the period. A running total that ends at the article date is current,
   however long ago it started ("since January, 170 tested positive"). An item that ended 31 to 45
   days before is borderline; older than that is past.
 Check 2. Is its period a completed calendar year or a completed flu season? If yes, it is past,
   even if it ended inside 30 days (an article from 5 January with "last year, 2,200 cases").
   Finished: "last year", "in 2015", "last season", "last winter", "the 2017-18 season", "the season
   ended with 300 deaths". Still running, so it passes: "so far this year", "this season", "since
   September 1", "to date". A present statement that the season is over ("the peak season is
   over") is a current trend and passes. Check 2 is only about a whole year or season: "last month"
   and "over the last three months" only need check 1.
 Examples:
   article 2016-01-05, "Last year, the city reported 2,200 cases": finished year, drop.
   article 2015-12-20, "So far this year, 2,200 cases": still running, keep.
   article 2017-07-10, "88 cases last month": ended 10 days before, keep.
   article 2015-08-03, "209 patients, January to March": ended 125 days before, drop.
   article 2016-01-29, "The peak season of the flu is over": a statement about today, keep (trend).
 - A comparison with the period just before ("2,000 cases this week, compared with 1,024 the
   previous week") keeps both numbers, if each passes the checks. A comparison with last year or an
   earlier season is not an item ("compared with 6,449 in the same week in 2017" drops the 6,449).
 - An old case announced late is dropped when the event itself ended more than 30 days before the
   article.

STEP 4. ITEMS.
Write one item for each distinct statement. Copy the article's facts. Never guess.
Fields: kind, strain, number, status, total_or_new, city, state, country, start_date, end_date,
words, evidence.

Kinds. Exactly four: "cases", "deaths", "hospitalized", "trend".
 - deaths: the article links the death to flu. If the person had confirmed flu but the cause of death
   is unknown or "yet to be categorised", write a cases item and no deaths item. If the article does
   not show the person had flu, leave the death out.
 - hospitalized: admitted, in hospital, under treatment in a hospital, in intensive care, or isolated in
   a hospital ("eight patients are undergoing treatment at Kasturba Hospital" is hospitalized). The item
   records the event of admission: start_date and end_date are both the admission day, not the length of
   the stay (if the admission day is not given, use the day of the report). Emergency visits and people
   merely "affected" or "sick" with no hospital named are cases.
 - trend: what flu is doing: rising, falling, stable, low, declared, ongoing.
 - A statement with no count is a trend. "People are dying of swine flu in Gujarat" is a trend in
   Gujarat, not a deaths item with no number.
 - "No cases yet" is a trend, never a count of 0.

strain: the article's own term for THIS item ("swine flu", "H1N1", "influenza A", "bird flu"). If the
  same sentence gives both a name and a subtype code, use the code. If the article gives two strains
  for the same item, write them as it does ("Influenza A & B"). Never list strains from elsewhere in
  the article. Do not correct the article. null if none.
number: a whole number, only if the article gives one. "More than 120" is 120. Vague amounts ("a
  couple of deaths", "nearly three dozen", "dozens") are not numbers: the statement becomes a trend.
  Never add or subtract numbers. A percentage or ratio is never a number; it goes in words. null for
  trend items.
status:
  Rule for cases and deaths: if the sentence names a strain ("swine flu", "H1N1", "bird flu", "H5N1"),
    the status is "confirmed", also for tolls and totals ("swine flu claimed 48 lives", "seven deaths and
    142 cases"). Status is null only when the sentence says just "flu" or "influenza" with no strain and no
    test word. Suspected beats both.
  deaths: "confirmed" when a strain is named for the death ("died of swine flu", "an H1N1 death");
    "suspected" when the article says the death is suspected or still being investigated; otherwise
    null ("the first flu death of the season").
  cases: "confirmed" when a named strain is reported ("a case of swine flu"), when the text says
    confirmed, lab-confirmed, tested positive or diagnosed, or when it says a named person has the
    flu ("is battling the flu", "is suffering from swine flu"); "suspected" for "suspected", "on
    suspicion", "samples sent", "report awaited", or flu-like illness with no other cause named;
    otherwise null ("416 reports of flu cases").
  hospitalized: "suspected" when the article says the patients are suspected ("isolated on
    suspicion") or they have flu-like illness; otherwise null.
  trend: always null.
total_or_new: "total" for running totals ("so far", "since", "to date", "a toll") AND for any count
  over a stated period of more than one day ("last week", "last month", "the week ending 7 May", "in
  the past 10 days"). "new" for a single newly reported event, even when its day is only known to a
  period ("a child died last week"), and for a count that falls on one day ("182 students sick on
  Thursday"). null for trend items.
words: trend items only; null otherwise. Use the article's own phrase when it has one ("seasonal
  influenza is increasing statewide", "rose by 38 per cent"). When the trend runs over several
  sentences, write a short plain summary ("no cases received so far this season"). A summary must not
  add facts.

Counting each thing once. Do not write the same fact twice. Record the headline, not the breakdown.
 - Headline first. For each place the article reports on, write the main total (cases, deaths,
   hospitalized) and, when the article gives it, the count that is new today, yesterday or in the last
   24 hours. That is usually one to three items per kind.
 - Do NOT write a breakdown of the headline: a list of districts, cities, counties, hospitals or age
   groups under a state or national total ("Lehigh County 120, Montgomery 93, Schuylkill 15, ..."), or the
   rows of a table. Write a part only when the sentence singles it out as its own news (it has its own
   "new" count: "seven in the last 24 hours"; or the article is about that place alone and gives no larger
   total).
 - Different countries are separate items, because they are not parts of one total. Keep one item per
   country the article reports on.
 - If the article gives both a weekly count and a running total for the same place, write both (each
   with its own period). Never add or subtract numbers to make a headline.
 - One patient, same day: keep only the most severe kind (deaths over hospitalized over cases). On
   different days, write separate items.
 - A "new" count inside a larger total is kept when it has its own period: "around 60 tested positive in
   July, including seven in the last 24 hours" gives cases 60 (total) and cases 7 (new). "A tourist died
   on Wednesday as the state toll rose to more than 120" gives deaths 1 (new) and deaths 120 (total).
   total_or_new tells them apart. A city count that is only a breakdown of the state count is not kept
   (see "Headline first").
 - Drop an item only when it repeats another item: the same kind, the same place, the same period and
   the same meaning.
 - Trend items: write ONE trend item for each distinct meaning, for each place and period. Write one for
   each vague amount the article ties to flu ("several deaths have been reported" gives a trend in that
   place). Do not write a trend for each row of a ranking or index table, and do not write a trend for a
   past season. If two
   trend statements say the same thing in different words for the same place and period (for example
   "mild" and "lower than usual" in one sentence, or a headline repeating the body), keep the one with
   the clearest words and drop the others.
 - Separate places that are not parts of one total are separate items. "Two people in China and one
   person in Hong Kong died" gives deaths 2 (China) and deaths 1 (China, city Hong Kong). Never add
   them up.
 - Round-up pages: a line printed next to a flu item belongs to it only if it is about the same flu
   event.
 - Never record: vaccination figures, age shares, death rates ("half of those infected die"), patient
   details (age, home town), animal outbreaks, or other diseases' numbers.

STEP 5. PLACES.
 - country is always filled, and must be a real country. Write the United States as "USA" and other
   countries in full ("India", "Australia").
 - The country is the place of the illness. The country of the news outlet, the language of the
   article or a person's nationality does not place the person: a story from a Korean paper about a
   singer does not put him in Korea. If the article does not say where the illness is, there is no
   country, so there is no item.
 - Territories go under their country: Hong Kong and Macau are "China" (city "Hong Kong", "Macau");
   Guam and American Samoa are "USA"; French Polynesia and New Caledonia are "France".
 - No country, no item. Drop worldwide and regional items. Never put "global", "worldwide", a
   continent or a hemisphere in a place field.
 - Fill in every part of city, state and country that you can work out with certainty from the places
   the article names (Houston gives Texas, Kolkata gives West Bengal, Brisbane gives Queensland and
   Australia). Leave a part null when you cannot be certain.
 - state: a real state or province, or null.
 - city: a real city only, where the cases or the hospital are, not the patient's home town. A suburb
   or a hospital name is not a city: use the city it belongs to (Westmead Children's Hospital gives
   "Sydney"). A county is not a city unless a city of the same name exists. A region ("Central
   Coast") is not a city. Otherwise null.
 - A national agency (the CDC, a ministry of health) gives the country and no state.

STEP 6. DATES.
start_date and end_date are YYYY-MM-DD. start_date is never after end_date, and end_date is never
after the article date. Work them out from the article date. If a date cannot be worked out, set both
to null.
 - A single day (an event, a death, "yesterday", "on Tuesday"): start and end are the same day. Never
   leave start_date null when end_date is a single day.
 - A newly reported case or event with no time wording: the article date for both.
 - "In the last 24 hours": the day before the article date, for both. "In the past N days" (a count):
   the N days ending the day before the article date (article 2023-01-03, "in the past 10 days" is
   2022-12-24 to 2023-01-02).
 - "Today": the article date. "Yesterday": the article date minus 1.
 - "On Tuesday" or "last Tuesday": the most recent Tuesday ON OR BEFORE the article date. If the
   article states the date of that day, use it.
 - "Last week": Monday to Sunday of the week before the article's week.
 - "This week" or "earlier this week" for one event: the Monday of that week, start and end the same.
 - "In the last week" or "in the past week" for a count or a rise: the Monday of the article's week to
   the article date.
 - "The week ending July 8": the 7 days ending that day. "The past N weeks": the article date minus N
   weeks, to the article date. "About two weeks back": the article date minus 14 days, for both dates.
 - "In July": the whole month. "Last month": the whole previous calendar month. "Over the last three
   months": the three full calendar months before the article's month. "The first fortnight of
   April": April 1 to April 14.
 - "Since April 3": that date to the article date. "Since March": March 1 to the article date. "Since
   last week": the Monday of last week to the article date. "As of Oct. 8", "till Monday": end is that
   day, start null (unless the text says "this year").
 - "So far this year", "since January", "year to date": January 1 of the article's year to the
   article date. Use an earlier end date only when the article gives its own as-of date ("as of
   Oct. 8", "till Monday", "the week ending Oct. 27").
 - "This season", "so far this season": start null, end is the article date. A season shown to be
   active is a trend ending on the article date.
 - A week number ("EpiWeek 18"): only if the article states that week's calendar range. Copy that
   range and add the sentence that gives it to the evidence. Never work a week's dates out yourself.

STEP 7. EVIDENCE.
evidence is a list of strings, each copied word for word from the article: whole sentences, with
typos, odd spacing and broken characters as printed. Put the parts in article order. Never join
sentences yourself. If the place or the date comes from a different sentence (a headline, a
dateline), add that sentence too. If you inferred a city, state or country that is not in the item's
own sentence, add the sentence that supports it. Do not add sentences that only give background,
forecasts or past numbers.

STEP 8. SELF-CHECK, then reply with the JSON object only. Check that:
 - every item is current (step 3), about flu in people, in a named country
 - no item comes from a teaser or related-link line, a ranking table, an opinion or a past-tense
   look-back
 - no breakdown of a larger total was written; hospital-treatment statements are "hospitalized"
 - every cases or deaths item whose sentence names a strain has status "confirmed"
 - no single-day item has a null start_date
 - every evidence part is found word for word in the article
 - the only kinds are cases, deaths, hospitalized and trend; trend items have number, status and
   total_or_new null; words is null except on trend items
 - no number comes from a percentage, a vague amount, a forecast, a finished year or season, or
   another disease
 - no fact is written twice; no two trend items repeat the same meaning for the same place and period
 - every place field is a real country, state or city; territories use their country
 - start_date is not after end_date, and end_date is not after the article date
 - if useful is false, items is an empty list; if useful is true, items is not empty
