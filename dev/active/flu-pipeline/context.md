# Context: decisions that aren't obvious from the code

## Why flu over dengue

The original scoping picked dengue as primary because it has genuine subnational ground truth (InfoDengue, municipality-level). A feasibility check found GDELT's own location tagging never resolved below country level for Brazil dengue stories (0/28 sampled locations were subnational), even at the exact week of a known 2019 São Paulo outbreak peak — so dengue's subnational ground truth had no matching subnational *signal* in the informal source, and wasn't a mapping differentiator after all. Flu became primary because it directly extends Ganser et al.'s own experimental setup (the strongest literature anchor), has near-global FluNet coverage vs. dengue/PAHO's Americas-only reach, and content-depth was confirmed across all 8 stratified flu countries (88%/88%) vs. dengue's narrower testing. Dengue is now a secondary/paused track, not a co-equal build — running both as parallel full pipelines was more scope than the timeline supports.

## Why Brazil/Indonesia/Kenya are excluded from the content-based comparison but kept in the frequency baseline (resolved decision)

A 5-day extended feasibility sample (10 URLs/day x 5 days x 2 countries, up to 100 articles total) first found Brazil/Indonesia's GDELT flu content was thin: 33% retrievable/content-rich, vs. the project's own >=60%/>=50% bars and the 88%/88% aggregate across the other 6 countries — consistent with Ganser et al.'s documented income/media-density coverage bias, not a single-day fluke.

That estimate has since been superseded by a **full-set check on real full-window data**, once BRA/IDN's Step 2 ingestion (593 BRA + 216 IDN matched articles across all their defined-onset seasons, 2015-2024) was complete: `investigation/check_bra_idn_full_retrievability.py` fetched *every* one of those 809 already-matched articles (not a small sample) using the same production `fetch_article_text` (`pipeline/gdelt/article_fetcher.py`, retrievable = `len(text.strip()) > 200`) and the same content-marker scoring used by every earlier `check_gdelt_flu_content*.py` script in this project, for a same-method, full-N confirmation:

| | Retrievable | Content-rich |
|---|---|---|
| BRA (593 matched) | 227 (38.3%) | 193 (32.5%) |
| IDN (216 matched) | 100 (46.3%) | 92 (42.6%) |
| Combined (809 matched) | 327 (40.4%) | 285 (35.2%) |

Both countries individually, and the combined figure, land below **both** the >=60% retrievability and >=50% content-depth bars — the small-sample estimate was directionally right, and the real numbers land somewhat higher (40.4%/35.2% vs. the sample's 33%) but still decisively short of the bar on both dimensions.

**Decision for BRA/IDN (final, per the project's own pre-committed rule)**: Brazil and Indonesia are **excluded from the content-based detection comparison** — they do not proceed to Step 3 LLM extraction or the Step 6 content-signal detection/lead-time test — but they **remain in the frequency-count baseline** (Ganser et al.'s comparison point: raw regex-matched article counts per week, no LLM and no requirement that the underlying article text be usable), since that baseline only needs a matched-URL count, not readable content. `pipeline/config.py`'s `included_in_gdelt_cohort=True` for BRA/IDN reflects their inclusion in ground truth and the frequency baseline; their absence from the content-comparison track is a reporting/pipeline-stage decision layered on top of that flag, not a change to it. Brazil/Indonesia's Step 1 ground truth and Step 2 matched-URL counts stay fully computed and on disk either way.

### Kenya fails the same bar, and worse — plus a real content-matching contamination

Kenya's 163 matched articles (8 defined-onset seasons, 2015-2024) were not covered by the BRA/IDN check above and were initially left in the content-comparison track by default on the strength of AUS/IND's numbers, without being checked against the project's own rule. That was an oversight, caught and corrected before Step 3 extraction began on Kenya's full dataset. Running the identical full-set check (`investigation/check_ken_full_retrievability.py`, same `fetch_article_text` + content-marker scoring as BRA/IDN, all 163 matched URLs, no sampling) gives:

| | Retrievable | Content-rich |
|---|---|---|
| KEN (163 matched) | 55 (33.7%) | 36 (22.1%) |

(Live-fetch numbers vary by a handful of articles run-to-run as syndicate URLs go up/down; a repeat run saw 54/35 instead of 55/36 -- the conclusion is unaffected either way.) Both figures are below the >=60%/>=50% bars, and materially worse than BRA (38.3%/32.5%) and IDN (46.3%/42.6%) — Kenya is the thinnest of the three, not the exception.

**A second, independent problem makes even that ~22% figure an overstatement of genuine signal**: inspecting the underlying matches found that **117 of Kenya's 163 total matched articles across the entire 2015-2024 decade (71.8%) are the exact same AP wire story** ("More hospitals are requiring masks as flu and COVID-19 cases surge," a January 2024 US domestic story), independently syndicated across 100+ different local-news URLs (Airdrie City View, Buffalo News, dozens of US TV-affiliate sites, etc.) — none with any Kenya-specific content. This is not a duplicate-write bug (each URL is genuinely distinct, unlike the USA 2023/2024 manifest-overlap issue above) — GDELT's own location tagging attributed Kenya as a matched location to each of these independently-crawled GKG records for a story that has nothing to do with Kenya. Of Kenya's 36 nominally "content-rich" matches, 21 are this same contaminating wire story (its "hospitals... cases surge" language trips the keyword-based content-marker scorer despite zero Kenya relevance) — leaving only **15 non-wire-story content-rich matches, of which roughly 6 are genuinely Kenya-specific outlets/content** (`standardmedia.co.ke`, `businessdailyafrica.com`, `kenyans.co.ke`, `capitalfm.co.ke` — Nairobi disease-burden reporting, a Kenyan health-ministry statement, KNH patient reporting, a former president's hospitalization) spread across the full decade. That is well under one genuinely-Kenya-specific, usable article per season — nowhere near enough for any per-season baseline/crossing computation in Step 6.

**Decision for Kenya (final, per the same pre-committed rule, applied without exception)**: Kenya is **excluded from the content-based detection comparison** for the same reason as BRA/IDN (fails both the >=60%/>=50% bars on the standard check), **and independently confirmed by the wire-story contamination finding** that even its nominal "content-rich" figure overstates genuine Kenya-specific signal by more than 3x. Kenya **remains in the frequency-count baseline** — the same reasoning as BRA/IDN applies (raw matched-URL counts don't require the content to be Kenya-specific *text*, only a country-tagged match), though the wire-story contamination means even Kenya's frequency-count series should be read with this caveat: a meaningful fraction of its "matched articles per week" signal in early 2024 is one non-Kenya viral story, not a Kenya-specific reporting increase. This is disclosed here rather than silently absorbed into the baseline.

### New consequence that needs a team decision: the content-comparison cohort has dropped below the project's own ≥40-unit bar

With BRA (9 seasons), IDN (10 seasons), and now KEN (8 seasons) all excluded from the content-based comparison, only **USA (9) + AUS (8) + IND (10) = 27 country-season units** remain for Step 3/6 content-signal work. The project's own pre-committed go/no-go bar for this cohort (`investigation/report.md`, `investigation/updated_problem_statement.md`) is **>=40 country-season units** — 27 falls decisively below it. This was not true before Kenya's numbers were checked (USA+AUS+IND+KEN = 35, still short of 40 but closer) and is a materially different situation from the BRA/IDN-only exclusion, which still left the cohort comfortably clear of the bar.

This is flagged here as an **open decision for the team**, not resolved unilaterally in this pass, options include: (a) accept the smaller 27-unit cohort and disclose it as a limitation narrower than originally scoped; (b) revisit GBR and/or JPN for the content-comparison track specifically (they were dropped for onset-*method* uniformity with the frequency-baseline countries, not for content-quality reasons — GBR has 3,707 and JPN has 3,000 already-ingested matched articles on disk from before the cohort change, so re-including either for content-comparison-only would not require new Step 2 ingestion, though it would reintroduce the UKHSA/peak-count-proxy asymmetry the cohort change was meant to avoid); (c) lean the write-up more heavily on IND (the largest remaining block) with appropriate small-cohort caveats on USA/AUS. See `tasks.md` for the same flag in the task-tracking record.

## Why Japan uses a peak-count proxy

FluNet's Japan records have no `SPEC_PROCESSED_NB` (specimen-processed denominator) — confirmed empirically (every JPN row returns an empty string for that field), not inferred from docs. Without a denominator, the flat >=10%-positivity-for-2-weeks rule used for every other country can't be computed. Decision: for Japan only, treat the single week with the peak raw `INF_ALL` count as the onset week (`onset_method="peak_count_proxy"`, `data_quality_flag="proxy_lower_rigor"`). No better free data source was pursued given the project timeline. Every output row carries this flag so Japan's onset is never silently presented as equivalent rigor to the positivity-based countries.

## Why UK uses UKHSA, and why it's labeled "England," not "UK"

WHO FluNet has literally zero records for GBR in any year (confirmed live, not just in the original spike). UKHSA's own dashboard (`api.ukhsa-dashboard.data.gov.uk`, metric `influenza_testing_positivityByWeek`) provides the same test-positivity metric, but only for **England** — not Scotland, Wales, or Northern Ireland. The live endpoint also defaults to returning per-age-band breakdowns (`age=65-79`, `age=45-64`, etc.), not the aggregate — this wasn't obvious from the previously-saved spike JSON, which had already been filtered to `age=all`. `ground_truth/ukhsa_client.py` explicitly filters `age=all&sex=all&stratum=default` server-side; getting this wrong would have silently 5x-10x'd the apparent weekly volume. Every UKHSA-sourced row's geography is "England," and this is never equated with "UK" in output or reporting.

## Why the round-robin model pool exists

Originally assumed Groq's constraint was a 30 RPM cap (a guess, not verified). Live testing (2026-09-13) showed the real constraint is a **1,000 requests/day (RPD) cap**, confirmed via the `x-ratelimit-*` response headers (reset-time spacing of exactly 86.4s = 86400s/1000 per call is the signature of a daily rolling window, not a per-minute one). At USA 2023's pilot volume alone (6,179 matched articles, roughly half reaching the LLM after the prefilter), a single model would take 5-6 real days.

Further testing found the RPD cap is tracked **per model**, not account-wide (a burst of calls to one model didn't move another model's `remaining-requests` counter). Of the models in this account's catalog, only three support `response_format=json_schema` (the structured-output mode the schema validation depends on): `openai/gpt-oss-120b`, `openai/gpt-oss-20b`, `qwen/qwen3.8-27b` — all capped at 1,000 RPD each. A fourth model, `allam-2-7b`, has a 7,000 RPD cap but flatly rejects `json_schema` responses (hard capability gap, not a quality tradeoff) — not usable. `extraction/multi_model_client.py` waterfalls across the three usable models.

**Per-model spot-check (done):** compared old-prompt vs. new-prompt extractions on the same 6 USA articles, spread across gpt-oss-120b and gpt-oss-20b (the pool naturally used both during testing; qwen3.8-27b wasn't hit in this particular sample). All 6 agreed on `relevant`; severity/strain showed only minor stochastic variance (e.g. "unspecified" vs "not_mentioned" -- same practical meaning). No model in the sample looked systematically weaker. One extraction failed transiently and succeeded on immediate retry with the same model/prompt -- ordinary API flakiness, not a quality issue.

## The real bottleneck is tokens/day (TPD), not requests/day (RPD) -- and it changes the full-build timeline materially

The 1,000 RPD cap above turned out not to be the binding constraint. Groq also enforces a **200,000 tokens/day (TPD) cap, per model**, confirmed by directly triggering it: `"Rate limit reached for model openai/gpt-oss-120b ... on tokens per day (TPD): Limit 200000, Used 199121, Requested 3372. Please try again in 17m56s."` -- a rolling window (like RPD), not a hard once-a-day reset. Our original prompt (6,000-char articles + 5 verbose few-shot examples, no `max_tokens` cap on the completion) used ~3,000-3,400 tokens/call, so **TPD, not RPD, is what a real run hits first**: 200,000 / 3,000 ≈ only ~65 calls/model/day -- not the ~1,000 RPD implied. This is why "all 3 pooled models exhausted their quota" happened after just 86 calls in one run, not the ~3,000 the RPD-only estimate predicted.

**Fix**: cut `MAX_ARTICLE_CHARS` 6000->1500 (news articles front-load key facts) and tightened the few-shot prompt (terser examples, compact JSON, shorter header) without dropping any of the 5 judgment categories; added an explicit `max_tokens=300` cap on completions (previously unbounded). Measured real result via the API's own `usage` field: **741 prompt tokens for a realistic 1,500-char article, vs. ~3,372 before -- a ~78% reduction.** New effective throughput: ~247 calls/model/day, ~741/day pooled across 3 models (vs. ~65/day and ~195/day pooled before).

**Full 6-country projection (done, flagged plainly as asked)**: using USA's own real measured match rate (6,179 matches / 85-day window = 72.7/day) as the primary anchor, the spike's per-country relative rates (UK/Japan/Australia) calibrated against that anchor, Kenya's real rate (8/85 days), and a flagged rough guess for India (no raw-mirror data point exists for it yet) --

| | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Total matched articles, full 6-country cohort (51 country-seasons) | ~74,000 |
| Estimated LLM calls needed (USA's own observed 69% reach-LLM rate) | ~51,000 |
| Time at the *old* prompt (~195 calls/day pooled) | ~263 days (~8.8 months) |
| Time at the *new shrunk* prompt (~741 calls/day pooled) | ~69 days (~2.3 months) |

Even after the ~3.8x token-efficiency fix, **a full 6-country run is ~2-3 months of continuous daily extraction on Groq's free tier** -- dominated by the USA alone (~75% of total volume). This is a volume-vs-free-tier-quota mismatch that further prompt engineering won't solve. **Explicit decision point for the team**: either accept a multi-month timeline, scope the full build down (e.g. fewer countries/seasons, or a stratified sample rather than every matched article), or move to a paid tier / different provider for the full run. Not resolved here -- flagged for a deliberate decision before committing to the full 6-country build. Pipeline work is **paused** pending this decision (see `tasks.md`).

Pricing confirmed via web search against third-party aggregator sites summarizing Groq's public rates (Groq's own pricing page is JS-rendered and didn't return content via direct fetch -- **verify these exact rates in the Groq console before committing budget**, but they're internally consistent across multiple independent sources):

## Why avian/animal flu had to be explicitly excluded

Pilot testing found ~36% of USA's matched articles were about H5N1/avian flu (2023's major poultry outbreak) — agricultural/trade news, not human seasonal flu. The word-boundary regex correctly matches "avian-flu" as a `flu`-containing URL token (working as designed), but the LLM initially marked at least one such article `relevant=True`, since the original 4 few-shot examples never covered this distinction. Fixed by adding a 5th few-shot example (poultry outbreak, no human cases -> `relevant=False`) and an explicit system-prompt instruction that `relevant=True` requires reported human cases. Verified against the real previously-misclassified article after the fix (`relevant` flipped to `False`). This is exactly the kind of issue only a real pilot run surfaces — worth remembering as a reason to always validate content-quality assumptions against real data before scaling up.

## Geotagging cross-check: two real bugs found building it, and a data-quality confound in validating it

Building the `primary_country` cross-check (see `tasks.md`) surfaced two genuine, previously-latent bugs -- neither obvious from a code read, both confirmed empirically against the live Groq API before being fixed.

**Bug 1: Groq's structured-output mode rejects a 3-way `anyOf`.** The first schema attempt typed the field as `CohortCountry | Literal["other"] | None`, which Pydantic renders as a 3-branch `anyOf` (an 8-value enum, a `const: "other"`, and `null`). Every real extraction call failed with `BadRequestError: json_validate_failed` -- 100% failure rate, not intermittent. Fixed by flattening to `Literal[CohortCountry, "other"] | None`, a single 9-value enum branch plus `null` (a 2-way `anyOf`, the same shape that `location_mentioned`'s existing `str | None` already uses successfully in production). Lesson: Groq's JSON-schema structured-output mode is pickier about `anyOf` shape than Pydantic's default rendering assumes -- a schema change should be smoke-tested against a live call, not just validated by Pydantic locally.

**Bug 2: gpt-oss-120b/20b are reasoning models, and `EXTRACTION_MAX_TOKENS=300` didn't budget for their reasoning tokens.** After fixing Bug 1, calls still failed the same way. Direct inspection of a raw completion (`response.usage.completion_tokens_details.reasoning_tokens`) showed the model spending 392 of 451 completion tokens on hidden reasoning before ever emitting JSON -- the new primary-country judgment call is harder than the original 5-field schema and pushed reasoning length past the fixed budget almost every time. Raising `EXTRACTION_MAX_TOKENS` would fix it but directly costs TPD budget (the same free-tier constraint already driving the multi-month full-build timeline above). Fixed instead with `reasoning_effort="low"` (`GROQ_REASONING_EFFORT` in `config.py`), confirmed empirically to keep completions inside the existing 300-token budget across all 3 pooled models (`qwen/qwen3.8-27b` isn't a reasoning model and accepts the unused parameter without error). Lesson: a schema/prompt change that makes the judgment harder can silently blow a token budget tuned for the old, easier judgment -- this is a real interaction between task difficulty and the reasoning-model/free-tier-quota constraint already documented above, not a one-off.

**A third finding, not a bug in this feature: presenting the GDELT tag as a stated fact anchors the model.** The original `f"Country: {country_hint}\nArticle: ..."` framing caused the model to agree with the given tag even against strong counter-evidence (the clearest test case, an article headlined about Australia's Northern Territory, was still classified as Brazil). Reframing to `f"GDELT-assigned country tag (verify, don't assume): {country_hint}\n..."` plus an explicit system-prompt sentence that the tag "is sometimes wrong" fixed this -- India's known Australian bird-flu cluster (`investigation/validate_geo_crosscheck.py`) went from being missed most of the time to being caught 89-94% across repeated runs, and the specificity guard (known-genuine outlets) stayed clean.

**A genuine data-quality confound found while validating Kenya's case, left unfixed (separate scope):** several of Kenya's wire-story URLs that survived link rot returned `fetch_article_text` output that is almost entirely site navigation and unrelated "Latest News" teasers from local-TV-station page templates (WKMG/ClickOrlando, Greenfield Reporter, WSLS, and others sharing a similar CMS) -- the real AP story body never appears within `MAX_ARTICLE_CHARS=1500` because the template interleaves a large amount of boilerplate between the byline and the actual article text. The model's "agree" verdicts on these specific URLs are a reasonable response to genuinely uninformative input, not a cross-check design flaw -- but it does mean Kenya's validation number understates the check's real accuracy on text that actually contains the story. This is a distinct problem from the already-known headless-browser/JS-rendering gap (that's about content missing from the raw HTML entirely; this is about `fetch_article_text`/`strip_html` extracting the wrong DOM region from HTML that does contain the real content) and is **not fixed here** -- flagged as a follow-up investigation, same treatment as the JS-rendering gap.

**Left as an explicit open decision, not resolved unilaterally**: USA's existing 218 and KEN's existing 8 Step 3 extraction records predate the `primary_country` field. Regenerating them means deleting/rewriting real production `data/extractions/{USA,KEN}.jsonl` entries -- treated as a decision for the team, not something to do silently in the same pass that built the feature. AUS and IND have zero prior extraction records, so their first real run already includes the field with no backfill question.

## Data-quality audit and fix cycle (2026-09-18)

A full-day audit of content-depth/retrievability numbers end to end, prompted by suspicion that the original feasibility spike's headline figures wouldn't hold up at production scale. Documented in the order found; several items here directly change or supersede numbers stated earlier in this file.

### 1. Silent fetch failures: missing User-Agent header

`get_with_retry()` (`pipeline/common/http.py`) sent no `User-Agent` header at all, which several mainstream news outlets (confirmed empirically: Daily Mail, Business Standard, Yahoo News, Zawya, iHeartRadio) either block outright or silently serve a stub/redirect page to. Fixed with a standard browser `User-Agent` default (`DEFAULT_USER_AGENT` in the same file), applied to every request. Not full bot-evasion, but it clears this specific, identified failure mode.

### 2. The original spike's 88%/88% figure re-audited and found compromised

The "Why flu over dengue" and BRA/IDN/KEN sections above both cite the original 8-country feasibility spike's 88%/88% retrievable/content-rich figure as a solid baseline. Re-auditing that original sample found it compromised two separate ways: USA's slice of the sample was 9/10 copies of one syndicated wire story (not 10 independent data points), and 2 of the 34 total sampled articles were mistagged to the wrong country entirely (a UK site's content labeled "Brazil," a UK tabloid labeled "Australia" -- the same class of geo-mismatch documented at length below, present from the very first spike). The 88%/88% figure should be read as directionally encouraging but not as a reliable baseline; it is not reliable enough to anchor decisions on its own, which is part of why the fuller audits below (full-set checks, then this session's Wave 1 re-run) were needed rather than trusting the spike figure as final.

### 3. USA's real production-scale numbers audited for the first time

Unlike BRA/IDN/KEN (see the full-set check above), USA's own matched set had never been audited at production scale before today -- only the spike sample. First production-scale check: **41.7% retrievable / 38.3% content-rich**, both below the project's own bars, with no wire-story or geo-mismatch contamination found in that pass (genuinely thin content, not an artifact). Note: a later re-run captured in `investigation/usa_full_audit_results.json` (an 800-URL sample of USA's full 58,458-article set) gives a different figure, 46.6%/42.75% -- both numbers are real audit outputs from different points in today's work; the discrepancy (plausibly the User-Agent fix in item 1 recovering some previously-blocked articles) was not fully reconciled in this pass. Treat 46.6%/42.75% as the more current figure since it's the one this session's Wave 1 re-run built on (see item 10).

### 4. Kenya's wire-story contamination

Kenya's matched articles were found to be 71.8% (117/163) one syndicated AP wire story mistagged to Kenya -- full detail already recorded above in "Kenya fails the same bar, and worse." Kenya excluded from the content-based comparison, kept only for the frequency baseline.

### 5. GBR and JPN re-checked for inclusion, neither re-included

The "New consequence that needs a team decision" section above flagged revisiting GBR/JPN for the content-comparison track (option b) once the cohort dropped to 27 units. Both were checked on their full already-ingested matched sets (`investigation/check_gbr_jpn_full_retrievability.py`): **GBR 46.0% retrievable / 40.9% content-rich** (narrowly short of both bars), **JPN 38.4% retrievable / 31.9% content-rich** (clearly short of both). Neither clears the bar; option (b) is closed. The cohort remains USA/AUS/IND for content comparison (27 units, still below the project's own >=40-unit bar -- see that section's still-open decision, now further complicated by item 10 below).

### 6. Geotagging cross-check built, unit-tested, and validated

Already documented in full above ("Geotagging cross-check: two real bugs found building it..."). Built as `primary_country` on the Step 3 schema, compared via `pipeline/extraction/geo_crosscheck.py::classify_geo_match`, validated against 3 known cases (Brazil/Australia mistag, Kenya wire story, India/Australia bird-flu cluster) at 89-94% catch rate with a clean specificity guard.

### 7. Content-richness heuristic replaced

`RETRIEVABLE_MIN_TEXT_LEN` (pure length) and `CHALLENGE_MARKERS` (keyword blocklist, `investigation/check_aus_ind_usa_full_audit.py`) were both unreliable at the boundary -- the keyword blocklist specifically was demonstrated to false-positive on genuine long articles (148 AUS articles flagged, sampled ones had 6,000+ real characters, because a stray challenge-adjacent phrase like "sign in" appeared inside otherwise-real prose). Replaced with `pipeline/gdelt/content_richness.py::score_content_richness` -- a combined structural score (length floor, sentence count, a length-normalized standardized type-token ratio, average sentence-chunk word count, plus the existing `CONTENT_MARKERS` overlap reused via import rather than re-copy-pasted). Validated by re-fetching all 148 originally-flagged AUS articles: 144 still live, **113/144 (78%) now correctly not penalized**. The remaining 31 were traced precisely: all pass every structural check cleanly (real sentence counts, real TTR) and fail only on `content_marker_hits == 0` -- confirmed via the *original* `aus_full_audit_results.json` that this was already true before any of today's changes, i.e. a pre-existing gap in the `CONTENT_MARKERS` word list itself (it never included "flu"/"influenza"), not a regression from the new scorer. Full validation script: `investigation/validate_content_richness_fix.py`.

### 8. Term-matching fix investigated and dropped

Investigated whether the flu-term filter's English-only, URL-only matching (`pipeline/gdelt/flu_filter.py`, `GkgRecord` carries only `url`+`country_codes`, no title/body parsed from the raw GKG record) was suppressing non-English coverage for BRA/IND/KEN.

- **BRA**: sampled 9 dates, one per BRA season, spanning the full 2015-2024 window (not a single-date fluke) -- of 135 distinct BR-FIPS-tagged candidate domains, **only 1 (0.6%) was genuinely Brazilian** (`folha.uol.com.br`, and even that one example was from its `/internacional/en/` English-language section, not domestic Portuguese content). BRA's problem is a candidate-pool/geo-tagging gap -- GDELT's `V2Locations` tagging for these sample dates essentially never surfaces genuine Brazilian domestic press as "Brazil" content at all -- not something any term-matching fix (in any language) can address.
- **IND**: genuine domestic Indian press is present in volume (`indianexpress.com`, `business-standard.com`, `post.jagran.com` -- a major Hindi-language publisher -- among 408 distinct domains sampled), but real sampled URLs from 5 such outlets, including Jagran itself, all use **English-language descriptive slugs regardless of source language**. A one-day full-96-stamp sample of IND's real FIPS-tagged pre-filter candidate pool found 4,838 unique candidate URLs; scaled to IND's full 850-day ingestion window (computed exactly from its 10 onset windows, zero overlap), that's **~4.1 million candidate URLs**. A real 40-URL fetch timing sample gave 8.85 URLs/sec at 16x concurrency -- fetching every candidate's title/body to check for native terms would take **~129 hours (~5.4 days) at 16x, ~86 hours (~3.6 days) even at 24x concurrency**, for IND alone. Ruled infeasible for this checkpoint.
- **KEN**: separately checked (see item 9's sibling finding) -- genuine Kenyan domestic press (`nation.co.ke`, `standardmedia.co.ke`, `tuko.co.ke`, and 6 other `.co.ke` domains) *is* present in the candidate pool, roughly 15-20% of KE-tagged records across an 8-date, full-window sample -- unlike BRA, this is not a candidate-pool-absence problem. KEN's actual limiting factor, per item 4 and the earlier full-set check, is thin absolute volume plus severe wire-story syndication contamination -- a different mechanism from BRA's, and also not something a term-matching fix addresses.

**Decision**: Phase 1 (native-language term matching) dropped entirely for this checkpoint. Both of its target countries (BRA, likely KEN) turn out to have deeper, different problems it can't touch, and the one country where the underlying premise held (IND has real domestic vernacular-adjacent press) was ruled out purely on fetch cost.

### 9. Comparative geo-heuristic built to replace the flawed presence-only version

The existing audit-scale geo heuristic (`investigation/geo_heuristic_recheck.py::geo_mentions_country`, a bare keyword-presence check, used for the "Audit-scale heuristic re-run" claim in `tasks.md`'s geotagging entry) was tested directly against the known India/Australia bird-flu cluster and **failed to catch it**: all 4 fetchable URLs from that cluster returned `mentions_IND=True`, because the article mentions India once in passing (patient's travel history) inside a story that mentions Australia 17 times. A bare presence check can't distinguish "mentioned once" from "what the article is about." **This means the previously-recorded claim that India clears both bars after geo-exclusion (61.6%/56.1%) was based on a heuristic that missed its own best-known test case, and should be treated as superseded -- see item 10.**

Fixed with `geo_matches_comparative()` (same file): the tagged country's keyword count must strictly exceed every other cohort country's count, not just be present. Validated with the same discipline as the original LLM-based cross-check, before trusting it: (1) **4/4** of the known India/Australia mismatch URLs now correctly excluded (Australia's 17-vs-1 count margin flips the verdict); (2) specificity guard against **22/22** fetchable genuine India articles from named domestic outlets (`thehindu.com`, `timesofindia`, `ndtv.com`, `hindustantimes.com`, `livemint.com`, `indiatoday.in`, etc. -- the same named-domain list `investigation/validate_geo_crosscheck.py` uses, not a random sample, per that script's own note that a random IND sample independently contains a second, different mistagged cluster) -- **zero over-exclusion**. Full validation script: `investigation/validate_geo_heuristic_comparative.py`.

## Why hourly-subsampling for Step 2 was rejected

The plan proposed a fallback: if full 96-files/day ingestion is too slow, try subsampling to 24 files/day (top-of-hour only) and adopt it if it retains >=90% of matched URLs found by the full method, on a few known days. Calibrated against 3 real days (USA 2023-12-11, GBR 2023-01-16, JPN 2023-11-27): recall was 31.7%, 0%, and 0% respectively — decisively failing the bar. News volume and specific flu stories cluster unevenly across a day's 96 windows; sparse-content countries (GBR, JPN) can miss every relevant article entirely under hourly sampling. Full 96-files/day ingestion is required; the tractability problem is solved via 8-way concurrency (measured ~2.9x speedup) and calendar-date-union deduplication across overlapping country-season windows, not via subsampling.
